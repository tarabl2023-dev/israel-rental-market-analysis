    How the estimates workData source, price levels and photo credits

    Comfortable is today's median asking rent for that town and size: the middle price of the flats for rent right now on Yad2, Madlan, OnMap, Homeless, Komo and Facebook, as collected by Realta (snapshot of ). Half sizes such as 3.5 rooms sit between the whole sizes either side. Where a town has fewer than 3 listings of a size, that size is estimated from its nearest size and from how rent rises with rooms across Israel.

    Why not the official CBS figure? CBS (Table 4.9) averages every lease signed in a quarter, including renewals of contracts that started years ago at lower rents. So it runs well below what you pay when you move in. For a 4-room flat in Jerusalem, CBS gives about ₪6,200 for , while the median listing today is about ₪9,100. Each price on the page shows the CBS average next to it as the official reference. Districts and Israel as a whole have no listing medians, so they use the CBS average brought up to asking rents with their big cities' market-to-CBS ratio.

    Listing sites don't publish the spread of prices either. Budget and luxury are rules of thumb around the median:

    • Budget: about 80% of the median (range 70-90%)
    • Comfortable: the median (range 90-115%)
    • Luxury: about 140% of the median (range 125-170%)

    Neighbourhoods. You can pick any neighbourhood with listings on Realta (about 1,000 across 94 towns). Each one's average asking rent is compared with its town's, and that ratio moves every price on the page. A neighbourhood with only a few ads is pulled towards the town average, so one unusual listing can't swing it. Neighbourhood averages mix all flat sizes, so an area full of small (or very large) flats can read a little low (or high). Which neighbourhoods count as "by the beach" is my local guide.

    Public transport. Station counts are train stations, light-rail stops and Haifa's Carmelit underground stations inside each city's boundary, from OpenStreetMap. Stations named after a city but just over its border (such as Kfar Saba–Nordau) count for that city. There's no official Israeli data on how rents change near stations. Studies of sale prices find homes near Tel Aviv's light rail rose faster (Globes), so the transport choice applies a rule of thumb: about +5% rent within walking distance of a station, and about −5% further out if you drive.

    What it will really cost. Everyday costs come from the CBS Household Expenditure Survey 2021 (Table 10), which reports what households spend in each sub-district. They're scaled to your household size with the CBS "standard persons" scale and brought to 2026 prices with the CBS price index for each group of goods. Every town uses its own sub-district (Herzliya uses Tel Aviv's, Ra'anana Sharon's, Givat Shmuel Petah Tikva's, and so on). Some survey cells are based on few households, and missing ones use the national average. Without a car, transport is the Rav-Kav nationwide monthly pass (₪315 per adult; a ₪139 bus-only pass exists for the periphery, see Rav-Kav Online). The survey's education average includes households without children, so with children it's doubled as a rough guide. Budget and luxury lifestyles scale eating out, leisure and clothing by 0.75 and 1.5. These are estimates of typical spending, not price quotes.

    Shabbat. A Shabbat meal is priced per person: about ₪45 at the budget level, ₪70 comfortable and ₪100 luxury, or the price you type in. These are my estimates. A basic supermarket Friday dinner (two challot, wine, a chicken, potatoes, salad, hummus, tahini, cola and a cake) cost ₪181–₪219 for 4–6 people in April 2026, depending on the chain (Oshek), and a fuller table with fish, soup, meat and desserts costs more; a ready-made Friday meal is about ₪120 a person. Your household already buys its ordinary food, so each family member adds the Shabbat price minus an ordinary meal (about ₪12, from CBS national grocery spending ÷ 90 meals a month) for each Friday dinner and Shabbat lunch you count. Each guest adds the full price at the meals you host. For context, the CBS survey (Table 13) shows households that keep Shabbat spend only about ₪27 more per person a month on Shabbat foods on average, partly because they eat out much less. That's an average over all households, so this page uses per-meal prices instead.

    Rent figures exclude arnona (municipal tax), building committee fees (va'ad bayit) and utilities, which the cost breakdown adds separately.

    My assumptions (not from official data), so you know what is an estimate:
    • Budget and luxury rent: about 80% and 140% of the median asking rent (the sites don't publish the spread of prices).
    • Room sizes with fewer than 3 listings in a town are estimated from its nearest size. Districts use the CBS average scaled up by their big cities' market-to-CBS ratio.
    • Transport choice: about +5% rent near a station, −5% further out if you drive.
    • Lifestyle: eating out, leisure and clothing ×0.75 (budget) or ×1.5 (luxury).
    • Education with children: twice the survey average, which includes households without children.
    • Neighbourhoods: prices move with each neighbourhood's average asking rent compared with its town's, pulled towards the town average when it has few ads. Which neighbourhoods are by the beach is my local guide.
    • Shabbat: about ₪45, ₪70 or ₪100 a person a meal for budget, comfortable and luxury (or your own price). Family members add that minus an ordinary meal (≈₪12), and each guest adds the full price.
    • Extras & treats (nails, massages, a premium gym and so on): rough typical Israeli prices that you can change. They're added on top of the survey averages, which already include some spending on leisure and personal care.
